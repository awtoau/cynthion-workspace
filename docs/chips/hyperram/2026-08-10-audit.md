# HyperRAM audit, 2026-08-10

What four external reviews and a day on the board established. Every finding
below is a fault in **our own instruments**, not in the W956A8.

**No measurement of the part is valid.** Not one figure this project has
recorded survives this audit. The reasons are now identified rather than
suspected, which they were not this morning.

## The faults, in order of how much they cost

### 1. Every DQS register write wrote `0x0000` — which is Deep Power Down

`hyperram_dqs_controller.py:249`:

```python
self.phy.dq.o.eq(Cat(Const(0, 16), ca[0:16]))
```

The 4:1 beat carries **four** bytes; the CA is **six**. So the second beat is
`CA[15:0]` plus two spare — and a register write has *zero* initial latency
(datasheet §9.2), so those spare bytes **are the word written**. They were a
constant zero.

`CR0 = 0x0000` means `CR0[15] = 0` = Deep Power Down (Table 10, tDPDIN 3 µs).
The part goes silent, the next read returns nothing, and `READ_DATA` hangs on it.

**The wedge and the "writes that changed nothing" were one event.** The ceiling
engine defaults to `dqs=True`, so its CR0/CR1 writes powered the part down and
then it hung. Fixed in `e6479e1`.

### 2. `RESET#` is wired to nothing on the non-DQS path

`gateware/board/cynthion_r1_4.py:210` declares a real pin, `PinsN("C1")`.
`hyperram_ceiling_top.py:556-558`:

```python
# The non-DQS PHY never drives RESET#; the platform's buffer holds it released
reset_assert = Signal()      # a dummy connected to nothing
```

Line 717 drives the reset heartbeat into that dummy. The sequence is a silent
no-op.

**Only a power cycle resets the external parts.** Neither reset scope we
actually use does:

| scope | how | gateware | CPU | external chips |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CPU reset | jump to `_start`, `ext_reset` | unchanged | reset | **unchanged** |
| FPGA reconfigure | Apollo, `soc_run.py` | reset | reset | **unchanged** |
| power cycle | unplug | reset | reset | reset |

So a part left in Deep Power Down by fault 1 stayed asleep across every
reconfigure. That is the leading explanation for a wedge that looked
intermittent and reconfigure-dependent — it depended on what the *previous*
bitstream left behind, which is not a property of the current one.

### 3. The DQS edge clock was on `CLKOS2`, which cannot reach an edge clock

Each bank ECLK input mux accepts ten sources: `CLKOP`/`CLKOS` from either left
PLL (4), four clock pads, two fabric taps. `CLKOS2` and `CLKOS3` are on the
**primary** clock network only, on any bank, on any ECP5. Evidence: the `ECLK_L`
tile's `bits.db`, muxes `W2_ECLKI0`, `W2_JECLKI1`, `S1W2_ECLKI0`,
`S1W2_JECLKI1` — the same ten on all four.

nextpnr could not route it and fell back to general fabric, announcing that as
`log_info` rather than a warning — so an unconstrained-skew clock carried every
capture-phase measurement, on a rig whose measurement *is* skew.

Two premises of the original diagnosis were wrong:

- *"~38 tiles apart, no PLL placement can reach it"* — false.
  `G_JLLCPLL0CLK{OP,OS}` is a die-level dedicated global; one hop. There was
  never a die to cross.
- *"`ECLKBRIDGECS` is the die-crossing bridge"* — false. Its bel pins are
  hardwired to two input-mux **outputs** (`.fixed_conn W2_JCLK0_ECLKBRIDGECS1
  W2_JECLKI1`, `W2_JCLK1_ECLKBRIDGECS1 S1W2_JECLKI1`; `E2_JECLKI0` /
  `S1E2_JECLKI0` on the right). Downstream of the input mux, so it can never
  rescue a source that mux rejects. `W2_CLKI0`/`W2_CLKI1`, which an earlier
  reading attributed to it, feed `CLKDIV` — see `E2_CLKI_CLKDIV0` in `ECLK_R`.

Fix: `CLKOS2` → `CLKOS`, delete the bridge. `ECLKBRIDGECS: 0/2` is the success
criterion, not 1/2. Verified by three builds and by the bitstream arcs
(`tmp/eclk-probe/{before,after}/top.config`: `arc: S1W2_ECLKI0 G_JLLQECLKCIB0`
→ `G_JLLCPLL0CLKOS`, and the one "general routing will be used" goes to zero).

Three generators can produce an edge clock, and all three are now on `CLKOS`:

| generator | domain | fixed in |
|---|---|---|
| `gateware/soc/hyperram_clocks.py` | `hr_fast` | `861439e` |
| `gateware/soc/clocks.py` | `fast` | `0498b36` |
| `gateware/probes/hyperram/hyperram_ceiling_top.py` | `fast` | this change |

The probe applet is what every ceiling sweep and published CK ladder was built
from, so **its** results are the ones #314 voids. `tests/test_eclk_sources.py`
asserts all three from the elaborated design, and reads the legal source list out
of prjtrellis rather than repeating it.

**The PLL must be pinned, and only the applet was missing it.** Every HyperRAM
pad is bank 7 (`iodb.json`, CABGA256: col 0, rows 2–11; `D1` = `LDQS8`,
`E2` = `LDQSN8`). The 25F tilegrid has `PLL0_LL`, `PLL1_LR`, `PLL0_LR` and no
upper-left site, so of the mux's four PLL sources only `G_JLLCPLL0CLK{OP,OS}`
exists — `X2/Y49/EHXPLL_LL` is the sole PLL that can drive a bank-7 edge clock.
Unpinned, a placer choosing a right-hand site drops the ECLK onto fabric silently.

Latent: `clocks.py` and `hyperram_clocks.py` both claim `X2/Y49` when they have an
edge clock, so `FLASH_PHY_FAST or HYPERRAM_DQS` **and** `HYPERRAM_BIST_DQS`
together will not place. Intended — two edge clocks on one die side cannot both be
served, and refusing the bel is the loud version of that.

Verified in the placed design, applet (7d88981) and SoC BIST DQS alike. From
`tmp/awto_soc/build/bist1-ck120-dqs1-mirror0-mirrordiv4`, tile `CIB_R25C2:ECLK_L`:

    arc: S1W2_ECLKI0 G_JLLCPLL0CLKOS      PLL CLKOS into the bank ECLK input mux
    arc: W2_JECLK0 W2_JNEIGHBORECLK0      onto bank 7's ECLK0 spine
    Promoted 'hr_fast_clk' to bank 7 ECLK0.
    Derived frequency constraint of 120.0 MHz for net hr_fast_clk$eclk7_0
    ECLKBRIDGECS: 0/2, ECLKSYNCB: 0/10, TRELLIS_ECLKBUF: 1/8
    "general routing will be used": 0

**nextpnr prints no Fmax and no Max-delay row for an ECLK domain** — every
consumer is a hard IOLOGIC or DQSBUFM bel with no timed arc — so the derived
constraint above is the whole of the tool's timing statement about it. Structure
is the only evidence available, which is why `scripts/soc_eclk_check.py --audit`
now runs inside every build (`soc_run.py`) and fails it on a fabric fallback.
Validated against the pre-fix design first: `--control` rebuilds the probe with
CLKOS2 and no `a_BEL`, and lands on `G_JLLQECLKCIB0` with one fabric fallback.

### 4. `READ_DATA` and `WRITE_DATA` are unbounded — STILL OPEN

They leave only when `final_word` coincides with an RWDS edge pattern. No
counter, no timeout, no abort. Simulated: 8 of 8 beats returns to `IDLE`,
**7 of 8 hangs for ever**. `bootram.py:801` has no escape either — the SoC's
`BUSY` state is `with m.If(psram.idle): m.next = "IDLE"` and nothing more.

This is the remaining blocker. It prevents testing whether register writes now
land, because the engine hangs in `READ` before a commanded run can complete.

### 5. `CR0[7:4]` is sparse, and the latency sweep only ever moves one side

Datasheet Table 8 (rev A01-006, p. 21): legal codes are **0** (5 clocks,
133 MHz), **1** (6, 166), **2** (7, 200, POR default), **14** (3, 83 MHz) and
**15** (4, 100). Codes **3–13 are RESERVED**.

At CK 85.7 the datasheet predicts pass = `{0, 1, 2, 15}`. We measure `{2, 6}`.
Now explained, and it is our instrument again:

**The controller's own wait is a build-time constant.** `HIGH_LATENCY_CLOCKS` is
fixed in `__init__` (`hyperram_controller.py:115`, `:147`) with no runtime input
— 14 by default on the non-DQS path, and `HYPERRAM_LATENCY_CLOCKS = 6`
(`bootram.py:95`) on the DQS path. `bist latency` reprograms `CR0[7:4]` in the
*part* and leaves the *controller* welded. Only the code whose part-side latency
matches the built-in count can align; the other fifteen fail regardless of how
good the part is. Sixteen rows, a "PASS set" and the datasheet's legal codes
printed beside them read as a test of Table 8. It cannot be one.

**And in the RESERVED range this part behaves as though `CR0[6]` is ignored by
the latency decode.** Pair `n` with `n+4` across the 2026-08-10 sweep (non-DQS,
CK 85.7143, build #137, controller held fixed so the part is the only variable):
0/4 = 2045 errors, 1/5 = 2047, 2/6 = **1**, 3/7 = 2043, 9/13 = 2047, 10/14 =
2043, 11/15 = 2047. Seven of eight pairs identical; 8/12 differs by 2 in a cell
already failing. So `6` was never a reserved code passing on its merits — it is
`2` with an ignored bit set, which is what made the old result look impossible.

Scope, because this is easy to overstate:

- **The bit is stored.** `0xefff` was written and read back with `[7:4] = 1111`.
  It is the decode that appears not to use it, not the register.
- **Codes 3–13 are RESERVED**, so this characterises undefined behaviour on one
  part. Diagnostically valuable — it retires the `{2, 6}` mystery — and worth
  nothing as a design assumption.
- **Not a defect.** A decoder built from a 3-bit comparison would produce exactly
  this, and "reserved" is the vendor declining to promise otherwise.
- The rival explanation, *"reserved codes are clamped to the default"*, is
  refuted: under it all of 3–13 would sit at 7 clocks and all would have passed
  the second run. Only 6 did.
- The datasheet's `clocks = 5 + sext4(code)` does not extend into the range
  either — it puts code 6 at 11 clocks, which would have failed against a
  controller waiting 7. It passed.

The write path is not the cause, which the code asserted until this run: CR0 was
commanded to `0xbf2f` then `0xefff` and read back as both, `[7:4] = 1111`
included.

**Both confirmed on the board once the controller swept too** (build with
`latency_clocks` driven from Table 8; prediction registered in #331 *before* the
run, and it hit):

| code | 0 | 1 | 2 | 6 | 14 | 15 | others |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| errors, welded controller | 2045 | 2047 | 1 | 1 | 2043 | 2047 | 2043–2047 |
| errors, swept controller | **1** | **1** | **1** | **1** | 2049 | **1** | unchanged |

- Codes 0, 1 and 15 went from ~2046 errors to 1. They were never the part's
  fault — the controller never moved with them.
- Code 6 is the only reserved code that aligns, because it decodes as 2 and the
  engine leaves reserved codes at the power-on count. Reserved behaviour, and
  useful only for explaining the old `{2, 6}`.
- Code 14 fails outright: 3 clocks is rated to 83 MHz against a 85.7 MHz clock.
  Over spec, so failing is correct behaviour.

### 5c. The CR0 readback was scored as a data word

`harness.check.eq(psram.read_ready)` had no state gate, and `read_ready` also
fires for the register read in `CONFIG_VERIFY`. So CR0 was counted and compared
against the golden data pattern: `words 2049` where 128 × 16 is 2048, and
`errors 1` at index 0 — before any data word could be wrong.

Same number in every otherwise-clean cell, which is what held `PASS set: []` on
a sweep whose five aligned codes were already good. Gated on the READ state,
#332.

### 5d. The DQS latency constant IS derivable, and the DQS read path cannot align

The "2x" in `CONTROLLER_CYCLES_PER_CLOCK` is the datasheet's **fixed-latency
doubling** (CR0[3] = 1 → the part waits `2 * L` CK), not a half-clock counter.
Getting that wrong is why the DQS constant looked arbitrary.

Cycle sizes, from the PHYs:

- non-DQS, `ODDRX1F` 2:1, 16 bits/cycle → **1 cycle = 1 CK**. `HIGH = 2 * L =`
  **14** for L = 7; `LOW = L = 7`. Both exactly the datasheet's pair.
- DQS, `ODDRX2F`/`ODDRX2DQA` 4:1, 32 bits = 4 bytes/cycle → **1 cycle = 2 CK**.
  `HANDLE_LATENCY` runs `latency_clocks + 1` cycles, so `latency_clocks = L - 1`
  = **6** for L = 7. Upstream's 5 is `L - 1` for L = 6, a different part's POR.
  `LOW_LATENCY_CLOCKS` cannot be exact for odd L — `L / 2` is not a whole cycle —
  and is dead while `fixed_latency` is True.

So the DQS path has an exact integral relation to Table 8 for every fixed-latency
code, contrary to the comment that said it had none. The sweep is not wired to it;
#314 voids DQS results first.

**And a structural misalignment no latency value can fix.** CA is 3 CK (odd; the
4:1 beat carries 4 bytes, so 6 CA bytes span 1.5 beats and the spare word is the
register-write data at CK 3). Fixed latency `2 * L` is even. So the first read
word always lands on an **odd** CK, while the controller's 32-bit beats always
start on an even one — a permanent one-device-word slip, independent of L and of
`readclksel`, which moves a sub-CK DQS phase.

That is exactly #186's measured *uniform one-word displacement* and *best 2/16*
over all sixteen phase × latency cells. Derived from source, **not measured** —
`ODDRX2DQA` D0 = `dq.o[31:24]` fixes the byte order, and the rest is parity. The
fix is a one-word bitslip in the PHY read path (LiteDRAM's ECP5 equivalent uses
`BitSlip(4)`), and the measurement that settles it is the capture buffer showing
the returned words displaced by exactly one, uniformly, at every latency code.
Whether `READCLKSEL` can absorb a whole CK is **not** established here — no
Lattice DDR-interface app note is in `sources/`.

### 6. #204 was fixed a year of debugging ago, and five files still cited it

*"JTAG readback slips a bit below a `sync`/TCK ratio of about four"* was quoted
in the present tense as live justification — including in comments written
during this audit.

The fault was three defects in **luna's** `JTAGCommandInterface`, not in JTAG: an
async TCK sampled through one flop, `jtag_tdi` sampled with no synchroniser, and
TDO combinational off `sync` registers so the host samples the *previous* bit —
the one-bit slip exactly. Luna's own SPI bridge gets it right, so
`JTAGCommandInterface` was the outlier.

Replaced 2026-08-07 by `gateware/probes/jtag_registers.py`, TCK-clocked
throughout. `jtck` closes at **295.68 MHz** against a 20 MHz constraint. No
ratio can enter a design clocked by TCK.

And **"~4" was never measured** — two board points, 5.0× clean and 2.5× slipped,
with 4 as the midpoint of an un-bisected interval. Simulation puts luna's real
boundary at 2.0×.

Corrected in `0a23283`. JTAG is usable again for applets and for an ILA readout.

## Things checked and found correct

Worth recording so nobody re-audits them:

- the `recovery_remaining` last-assignment-wins arrangement — 2–3 cycles of CS#
  high measured against a tCSHI minimum of 10 ns
- `CA[45]`/`CA[46]` — correct, though `CA[45]` is a caller-held invariant where
  LiteX and Lattice both hardcode it
- register **reads** correctly take the full `CR0[7:4]` latency
- `rwds.e.eq(~is_register)` correctly releases RWDS — Lattice, blackmesalabs and
  wtfuzz all get this wrong
- `T_CSHI_NS = 10.0` is right; tRWR is *not* an inter-transaction gap

## The pattern

Every fault that cost real time was **an instrument that could not report its
own failure**:

- a register declared and never bound, reading zero and believed
- `done` that could never assert on a completed run
- a config path never reachable after power-up
- an edge clock demoted to fabric, announced as `log_info`
- a `RESET#` driving a dummy signal
- a cell that compared 2 words of 32,768 and was scored `PASS`
- three diagnostics that crashed formatting `None` instead of printing what they
  had found
- a latency sweep that moves the part and not the controller, and prints the
  datasheet's legal codes next to a result that cannot test them
- a comparator that scores the part's own CR0 readback as a data word, so no
  cell can reach zero errors however good the part is
- and, inside the sim written to catch the latency fault, a check that measured
  `read_ready` — reporting the MODEL's latency, not the controller's, and
  reading a flat 18 cycles at every setting until it was moved onto the FSM

`bist smoke` grading *itself* — "WEDGED, this says nothing about the part" — is
the only reason several of these surfaced at all.
